The doctrine of equal pay for equal work, enshrined in Articles 14, 16, and 39 of the Indian Constitution, originally aimed to eliminate discriminatory pay scales between male and female employees who perform the same or equal tasks while working for the state government.
However, this principle has been interpreted more broadly to prevent discriminatory pay scales for employees of equal value and nature, regardless of sex, when no rational explanation for the discrepancy exists.
The government must safeguard marginalized groups from discriminatory actions, even if they voluntarily undertake unequal employment terms.
The principle of equal pay for equal work must be read as part of Article 14's tenets of equality, which permit differential classification based on reasonable grounds [Paras 18 and 19].
B.
The burden of proof falls on the person alleging discrimination under Articles 14 and 16 of the Indian Constitution.
However, when earlier-stage pay scale equivalence is conceded, the burden shifts to the employer to confirm justification for any subsequent pay scale disparity.
If employees prove an earlier parity and claim unjustified inequality, the burden of proof is discharged.
If the employer fails to provide evidence justifying any change and inequality, the action is deemed arbitrary and discriminatory [Paras 20 to 24].
C Indian Constitution's Article 226 and Industrial Disputes Act's Section 10(1)(d) safeguard equal pay for equal work.
In Letter Patent Appeal, the Single Judge's order, which set aside an award of equal pay for equal work based on evidence, was deemed to have a significant procedural irregularity, given that there was no justification on the record for the pay scale discrepancy between long-matched positions carrying equivalent pay scales [Para 23].
