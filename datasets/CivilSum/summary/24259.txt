According to Article 226 of the Constitution of India and Section 2(k) of the Industrial Disputes Act, a writ court should not entertain a writ and proceed with the adjudication of disputed facts where an effective alternate remedy is available to both the parties under the Industrial Disputes Act, especially when earlier writ was dismissed on the same ground.
Thus, for questions of disputed facts, such as whether the workers were on strike or absent from duty amounting to abandonment of job, a writ is not the appropriate forum.
Instead, Labour Tribunals can more effectively adjudicate and grant any appropriate relief which the High Court cannot.
[Paras 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16] B.
As per Article 226 of the Constitution of India, violation of provisions of Certified Standing Orders does not constitute a violation of statutory provisions, so as to enable the High Court to exercise its writ jurisdiction to interfere.
Certified Standing Orders are only "statutorily imposed conditions of service" and not statutory provisions themselves.
[Para 11] C. Under Sections 2(q) and 22 of the Industrial Disputes Act, legal and illegal strikes have different consequences.
Workmen on an illegal strike may be liable for disciplinary action but those on a legal strike may not be.
Going on an illegal strike may also constitute a break in service, and in the absence of proper notice of strike under Section 22, the workmen cannot even be deemed to be on strike but can certainly be considered on unauthorised absence from duty.
[Paras 14, 18, 19] D. According to Sections 2(q) and 22 of the Industrial Disputes Act, even if the act of workmen is a punishable offense under another statute relating to public utility services, the employer is not disentitled to take action under the Certified Standing Orders in the absence of proper notice of strike under Section 22.
