The Parliament has the power to legislate on the recognition and derecognition of teacher education institutions under Sections 14 and 15 of the National Council for Teacher Education Act, as the functions of the NCTE outlined in Sections 12, 14, 15 and 17(1) to (3) are within its legislative competence.
[Para 13]
Section 17(4) of the NCTE Act, which disqualifies teachers who have obtained qualifications from de-recognised institutions, for employment under Central or State Governments, is unconstitutional as it cannot force States or their funded institutions to employ only teachers with a specific qualification nor can it derecognise qualifications for employment purposes.
This sub-section can be severed from the rest of the Act without the need to declare the entire Act unconstitutional.
[Paras 13 to 17]
Those who obtained qualifications before the enforcement of a new law cannot be denied employment based on the de-recognition of their qualifications under the new law.
[Para 18]
The NCTE's de-recognition of the B.Ed.
(Vacation Course) as compared to the B.Ed.
(Regular Course) and the B.Ed.
(Distance/Correspondence Course) is not justified as the former involves face-to-face education like the latter.
[Paras 19 and 20]
The NCTE violated Section 14(3)(b) and natural justice principles by de-recognising the B.Ed.
(Vacation Course) without giving the institution an opportunity to make an effective representation.
Merely asking for information before forming an opinion is not a substitute for providing the opportunity to make a written representation as required by the proviso to Section 14(3)(b).
[Para 19]
The High Court has the power to direct the NCTE to recognise a course if both parties have been heard on merit and all objections to sustain an impugned order/de-recognition are found not sustainable without pointing out any other ground for remitting the case for reconsideration to the same authority.
