Article 226 of the Indian Constitution, Section 179 of the Army Act (1950), and Rules 22, 23, 24, and 180 of the Army Rules (1962) govern court martial proceedings.
Contention that the impugned order of conviction and sentence were not compliant with these rules cannot be reviewed.
The High Court held that, while it exercises powers of judicial review under Article 226, it cannot re-appreciate the evidence produced but held that the court martial proceedings were fair.
The petitioner was afforded every opportunity to defend his case, and the evidence against him sufficed to sustain a conviction under Section 302 of the Indian Penal Code.
The Court found no legal infirmity in the order of conviction and sentence passed by the court martial, warranting interference under Article 226 of the Constitution.
[Para 7]

Article 226 of the Indian Constitution, Section 179 of the Army Act (1950), Section 302 of the Indian Penal Code (IPC), and court martial proceedings highlight the differences between the plenitude of power available to High Courts under Article 226 and that available to a Court hearing an appeal against conviction.
The power of judicial review ensures that judicial and quasi-judicial tribunals exercising their quasi-judicial powers do not exceed their statutory jurisdiction and correctly administer the law laid down by the statute under which they act.
The judicial review is not, however, intended to ensure that the conclusion on which the tribunal reached is necessarily correct in the eye of the Court.
[Para 4]

The provisions of Section 197 of the Criminal Procedure Code (1973) do not apply to court martial proceedings as courts martial under the Army Act are courts in the strict sense.
